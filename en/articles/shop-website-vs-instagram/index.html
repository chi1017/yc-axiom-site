Does a small business need a website?
Or is Instagram enough?

Running a small business Website / Instagram About 10 min read Updated: 2026/11/03

For many small businesses, the first online presence is Instagram: it's free, you just post photos, and customers are used to browsing there. So do you need a website? It depends on how you want customers to find you. This article compares what each does best and gives you a step-by-step list, so you can decide whether you need one now and where to start.

Picture how a new customer finds a business

Say you run a dessert shop in Taichung. A customer who has never heard of you wants dessert this weekend. Typically they search Google for "best desserts Taichung" or "West District dessert shop," look at places on the map and a few recommendation articles, then open one or two websites to check hours and prices. More and more people also simply ask ChatGPT or Gemini, "Where's a good dessert place for afternoon tea in Taichung?"

Look at every stop on that path: search results, maps, articles and AI answers. Instagram posts rarely show up in any of them. A business with only Instagram works well for people who already follow it, but is nearly invisible to people who don't know it yet.

What Instagram does well: keeping followers up to date

Instagram's strengths are engagement and immediacy: followers quickly see new products, events and stories, and photos and short videos convey your atmosphere best. If most of your customers are regulars or come through friends, Instagram already does a lot, and there's no need to deny that.

Three blind spots of Instagram alone

1. Hard to find in search. When customers search Google for area + type of business, the top results are mostly maps, websites and review articles. Much Instagram content needs a login to see in full, so search engines can read only a little, and it rarely ranks high.

2. Information is scattered across posts. Customers have to scroll back post by post to find hours, prices, address, directions and how to book. Typical problems: a closure notice posted in a story three months ago that's long gone, or old posts still showing prices you've since changed. Customers who can't find information, or see outdated details, may simply go elsewhere.

3. Your content isn't entirely yours. If your account is hacked, wrongly suspended or hit by an algorithm change, your reach can drop overnight, and you have no control. Years of posts are also hard to move anywhere else intact.

What a website does well: being found, understood and kept

  • Found by search engines: every page has a clear title and description, so Google knows who you are, where you are and what you sell.
  • Understood by AI search: tools like ChatGPT and Gemini draw on clearly organized public information when recommending businesses, and a well-structured website is cited correctly more easily than scattered posts.
  • Everything in one place: hours, prices, FAQ and directions sit in fixed spots customers can find in three seconds.
  • Content you own: your address, content and data are in your hands, not subject to one platform's rules.

Not either-or: Instagram for engagement, a website for being found

The better approach is to use both: keep posting news and engaging regulars on Instagram, put information that rarely changes but customers must know on your website, and link to it from your Instagram bio. New customers arrive from search, get to know you on the website, then visit Instagram for the atmosphere; regulars tap through from Instagram to check hours or book. With clear roles, you also stop answering the same questions in comments over and over.

The minimum a small business website needs

A website doesn't need many pages. One page is fine, as long as the following are written clearly as text (not just as an image, since search engines and AI can't read text inside images):

  • A one-line introduction: area + type of business + what makes you special + who you're for. For example, "A handmade dessert shop in West District, Taichung, known for low-sugar pound cakes, ideal for afternoon tea and gifts."
  • Menu or prices: main items and price ranges, with seasonal items noted separately.
  • Hours, address and directions: with a map and the nearest parking or bus stop.
  • How to book or get in touch: phone, LINE or a booking link; pick one main method.
  • FAQ: pets, power outlets, private bookings; turn customers' common questions into Q&A.
  • Instagram and Google Maps links: so customers who want more photos can get there in one tap.

From Instagram only to a website: five steps

  1. Gather your information: collect the hours, prices and FAQs scattered across your posts and story highlights into one text document. This usually takes the longest, but you only do it once.
  2. Write your one-line introduction using the formula above, avoiding lines like "We put our heart into every dish" that any business could use.
  3. Choose how to go live: on a budget, start with a rental template; for special needs, build a custom site (see below).
  4. Put your website link everywhere: your Instagram bio, the website field of your Google Business Profile (your listing on Google Maps) and your LINE official account. Keep your name, address and phone identical everywhere.
  5. Tell Google once you're live: submit your site in Google Search Console (Google's free tool for telling it which pages you have), then check every quarter that your information is still correct.

When a small business can skip a website for now

If nearly all your customers are regulars or referrals, your details rarely change and you're not trying to attract new customers, a complete Google Business Profile may be enough for now. But if you want people who've never heard of you to find you in search, a website is worth it. Ask yourself three questions: have you had many new customers in the last three months? Do customers often message to ask your hours or prices? Do you appear when you search for your area + type of business? If the answers are "not many," "often" and "no," it's time for a website.

FAQ

I already have a Google Business Profile. Do I still need a website?

Your Google profile matters, but its fields are limited and can't hold a full menu, your story or an FAQ. It's best to have both, with your website address in the profile's website field.

Can a link-in-bio page (like Linktree) replace a website?

A link page is handy for sending people to your platforms, but it has very little content, and search engines almost never treat it as your business's main page. Use both, but it can't replace a website.

What if I don't have time to update a website?

Put only information that rarely changes on your website and keep posting news on Instagram. You usually only need to update it when hours or prices change, a few times a year.
